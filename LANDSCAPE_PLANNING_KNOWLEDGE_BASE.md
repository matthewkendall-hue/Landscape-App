## 1. Foundational Texts & Authors

### 1.1 Ian McHarg — *Design with Nature* (1969)

The most influential ecological planning text of the 20th century. McHarg pioneered the idea that human settlement must be designed **with** natural processes, not against them.

**Core Contributions:**

- **The Overlay / Layer-Cake Method** — McHarg layered transparent maps of natural features (geology, hydrology, soils, vegetation, wildlife, climate) to reveal land-use suitability. This process is the direct conceptual precursor to modern **GIS** (Geographic Information Systems).
- **Suitability Analysis** — A systematic framework for determining which land uses are ecologically appropriate for a given area, based on the composite overlay of environmental constraints and opportunities.
- **Multidisciplinary Integration** — Planning decisions must synthesize geology, hydrology, soil science, botany, zoology, and climatology into a holistic view.
- **Critique of Conventional Development** — McHarg argued that sprawling, nature-blind urban development causes flooding, erosion, pollution, and habitat destruction — all avoidable through ecological planning.

**Key Case Studies:**

| Case Study | Focus |
|---|---|
| **Staten Island** | Regional ecological inventory and suitability mapping |
| **The Valleys (Baltimore County)** | Demonstrating how development can follow natural drainage and woodland patterns |
| **Plan for the Potomac** | Watershed-scale ecological planning |

**Legacy:** McHarg's work established that landscape architects are not decorators but environmental stewards. His methods underpin modern environmental impact assessments, green infrastructure planning, and GIS-based land-use analysis worldwide.

---

### 1.2 John Ormsbee Simonds — *Landscape Architecture: A Manual of Site Planning and Design* (1961)

For over 50 years the foremost practical reference on the profession. Simonds provides systematic, step-by-step procedures from site analysis through detailed design.

**Core Contributions:**

- **Site Analysis → Programming → Schematic Design → Detail Design** — A clear procedural framework that made the profession's methodology accessible and teachable.
- **Spatial Design with Hand Sketches** — Hundreds of diagrams illustrating concepts of enclosure, movement, proportion, and human-scale design.
- **Topography & Grading** — Detailed treatment of working with contours, cut-and-fill calculations, and drainage design.
- **Sustainability & Growth Management** — Later editions expanded into urban sprawl reversal, open-space planning, regional greenways and blueways, and non-destructive site procedures.
- **Human Experience** — Emphasis on how people perceive, move through, and emotionally respond to designed outdoor spaces.

---

### 1.3 Frederick Law Olmsted — The Father of American Landscape Architecture

Olmsted established the philosophical and practical foundation of the profession in the 19th century. His six enduring design principles:

| # | Principle | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Genius of Place** | Design must respond to the unique characteristics of each site, including its disadvantages |
| 2 | **Unified Composition** | Every element must serve an overarching design purpose; avoid gratuitous decoration |
| 3 | **Orchestration of Movement** | Subtly guide circulation; separate traffic types for efficiency and safety |
| 4 | **Orchestration of Use** | Distribute activities into appropriate zones to prevent conflicts |
| 5 | **Sustainable Design & Conservation** | Use plants that thrive with minimal maintenance; conserve natural site features |
| 6 | **Comprehensive Approach** | Landscapes should extend benefits beyond their boundaries through connected greenways and parkways |

**Landmark Projects:** Central Park (NYC), Prospect Park (Brooklyn), The Emerald Necklace (Boston), Biltmore Estate (Asheville).

---

### 1.4 Garrett Eckbo — *Landscape for Living* (1950)

The book that defined **Modernist landscape architecture**. Eckbo rejected the old formal-vs-informal debate and asked: "Why not biology plus geometry?"

**Core Contributions:**

- **Landscape as Social Space** — A landscape is the site of the interaction of people and place; exterior spatial design is the purposeful formation of that interaction.
- **Rejection of Pure Naturalism** — Criticized Olmsted-era "palliative" park design; advocated for a scientific method free of preconceptions.
- **Mass, Void, Balance & Line** — Prioritized spatial composition principles over decorative rules.
- **Multidisciplinary Modernism** — Influenced by Walter Gropius and the Bauhaus; landscape design as a vehicle for social change.
- **Materials-First Thinking** — Thorough treatment of earth, rock, water, plants, and structural elements as the designer's palette.

---

### 1.5 Anne Whiston Spirn — *The Granite Garden: Urban Nature and Human Design* (1984)

Named one of the **100 most important books of the 20th century** by the American Planning Association. Credited with launching the **ecological urbanism** movement.

**Core Contributions:**

- **Nature Is Already in the City** — Cities are not separate from nature; they have their own air, water, geology, plant, and animal systems that must be understood and designed with.
- **Urban Ecology as Design Framework** — Integrated treatment of air quality, water management, urban soils, geology, and urban wildlife habitat.
- **Process over Form** — Warned that designers who focus only on form and materials, while ignoring the processes that shape landscapes during and after construction, produce designs that fail in the real world.

---

### 1.6 Geoffrey & Susan Jellicoe — *The Landscape of Man* (1975)

A sweeping historical survey of landscape design from ancient civilizations to the modern era, tracing how cultures have shaped land to reflect their values, beliefs, and social structures.

---

### 1.7 Other Essential Figures

| Author | Key Work | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| **Kevin Lynch** | *The Image of the City* (1960) | Defined how people perceive urban space: paths, edges, districts, nodes, landmarks |
| **Lawrence Halprin** | *The RSVP Cycles* (1969) | Participatory design process; pioneered movement-based design (Freeway Park, FDR Memorial) |
| **Sylvia Crowe** | *Garden Design* (1958) | English landscape tradition; integration of garden design with broader land use |
| **Richard Forman** | *Landscape Ecology* (1986) | Patch-Corridor-Matrix model for understanding land mosaics |

## 2. Ecological Planning

### 2.1 McHarg's Ecological Method

The ecological planning process follows these stages:

```
1. INVENTORY        → Collect data on all natural systems
2. OVERLAY          → Layer maps of each system transparently
3. ANALYSIS         → Identify constraints, opportunities, and conflicts
4. SUITABILITY      → Determine which uses are appropriate where
5. DESIGN           → Shape development to fit ecological capacity
```

**Data Layers for Overlay Analysis:**

- Bedrock geology & surficial geology
- Physiography & slope analysis
- Hydrology (surface water, groundwater, floodplains, wetlands)
- Soils (type, depth, drainage, bearing capacity, agricultural value)
- Vegetation (forest types, successional stages, rare communities)
- Wildlife habitats & movement corridors
- Climate & microclimate (sun, wind, precipitation patterns)
- Land use history & cultural resources
- Visual / scenic quality

### 2.2 Ecological Succession & Disturbance

Understanding natural change over time is fundamental to planting design and land management:

- **Primary succession** — Colonization of bare substrate (rock, sand)
- **Secondary succession** — Regrowth after disturbance (fire, clearing)
- **Climax community** — The stable endpoint community for a given climate and soil
- **Disturbance regimes** — Fire, flooding, wind, and grazing are natural processes that maintain diversity; good design accommodates rather than suppresses them

### 2.3 Watershed-Based Planning

Water is the organizing principle of landscape:

- Every site sits within a **watershed** (drainage basin)
- Development decisions upstream affect flooding, water quality, and habitat downstream
- Best practice: plan at the **watershed scale**, not the property-line scale
- Key strategies: preserve riparian buffers, manage stormwater at the source, protect recharge areas, daylight buried streams

### 2.4 Habitat Connectivity

- **Wildlife corridors** connect habitat patches and allow species movement, genetic exchange, and migration
- **Fragmentation** (roads, development) is one of the greatest threats to biodiversity
- **Riparian corridors** (streamside zones) serve as natural connectors
- Design principle: maintain and restore connections between large habitat areas

### 2.5 Native Plant Communities & Biomes

- Use **regionally native plants** adapted to local soils, climate, and wildlife
- Understand your site's **biome** (temperate forest, grassland, desert, etc.) and **plant hardiness zone**
- Design plant communities, not just individual plants — mimicking the **structure** (canopy, understory, shrub layer, ground layer) of natural communities
- Benefits: lower maintenance, better wildlife habitat, resilience to climate stress

## 3. Design Principles

### 3.1 Core Visual & Spatial Principles

| Principle | Definition | Application |
|---|---|---|
| **Unity** | All elements feel as one coherent composition | Repetition of materials, colors, forms; consistent design vocabulary |
| **Balance** | Visual weight is evenly distributed | *Symmetrical* (formal, mirror-image) or *Asymmetrical* (informal, achieved through contrast) |
| **Proportion** | Size relationships between all features | Relate plant masses, paving areas, and structures to human scale and to each other |
| **Rhythm** | Repetition creating visual movement | Repeated plantings, paving patterns, or structural elements that draw the eye forward |
| **Emphasis / Focal Point** | Visual hierarchy — what matters most | Specimen trees, water features, sculpture, or framed views |
| **Contrast** | Juxtaposition of opposites for visual interest | Texture (fine vs. coarse), color (warm vs. cool), form (upright vs. spreading) |
| **Simplicity** | Restraint in the number of elements and materials | Avoid visual clutter; a few well-chosen materials create stronger designs |

### 3.2 Spatial Enclosure & Outdoor Rooms

Borrowed from architecture, the concept of creating defined **outdoor rooms**:

- **Floor** — Ground plane (lawn, paving, groundcover, water)
- **Walls** — Vertical elements (hedges, walls, fences, shrub masses, berms)
- **Ceiling** — Overhead plane (tree canopy, pergola, arbor, sky)

**Degrees of Enclosure:**

```
Fully Enclosed  ←————————————————→  Fully Open
(courtyard)    (woodland glade)   (meadow)    (open field)
     ↑                                            ↑
  intimate,                                   expansive,
  sheltered                                   exposed
```

People feel most comfortable in spaces where enclosure is proportionate to the space's purpose — intimate for seating areas, more open for active recreation.

### 3.3 Sequence & Movement

Designing the experience of **moving through** a landscape over time:

- **Approach** — How one arrives; the transition from public to private
- **Threshold** — The moment of entry; a gateway, change in material, or spatial compression
- **Procession** — The journey through a series of spaces with varying character
- **Arrival / Climax** — The destination; often the most dramatic space or view
- **Conclusion** — The exit; a resolution or return

**Techniques:**
- Compression and release (narrow path opening to a broad vista)
- Concealment and revelation (hiding a view until a specific vantage point)
- Change of level (steps, ramps, bridges — each alters perception)
- Change of material (gravel → stone → lawn signals different zones)

### 3.4 Color Theory in Planting Design

- **Warm colors** (red, orange, yellow) — advance toward the viewer; energizing; best for focal points
- **Cool colors** (blue, violet, green) — recede; calming; create depth
- **Monochromatic schemes** — Variations of one hue; elegant and restful
- **Complementary schemes** — Opposite hues (e.g., purple + gold); vibrant and high-contrast
- **Analogous schemes** — Adjacent hues (e.g., blue + violet + magenta); harmonious
- **Foliage color** is more permanent than flower color and should form the design backbone
- **Seasonal shifts** — Plan for spring bloom, summer fullness, autumn color, and winter structure (bark, evergreens, seed heads)

### 3.5 Temporal Design — The Fourth Dimension

Landscapes change through:

| Timeframe | Design Consideration |
|---|---|
| **Hours** | Sun and shadow patterns, lighting, scent (night-blooming flowers) |
| **Seasons** | Bloom succession, foliage color, deciduous vs. evergreen, snow loading |
| **Years** | Plant growth and maturation; a 5-year landscape differs vastly from a 20-year one |
| **Decades** | Ecological succession; infrastructure aging; cultural shifts in use |

Design for the **long game** — select plants and materials that improve with age.

## 4. Placement & Site Analysis

### 4.1 Solar Orientation & Microclimate

**Sun:**
- **South-facing slopes** (Northern Hemisphere) receive maximum solar radiation — warmer, drier, earlier snowmelt
- **North-facing slopes** are cooler, moister, and support different plant communities
- **East-facing** exposures get gentle morning sun (ideal for many plants)
- **West-facing** exposures get intense afternoon heat (stress for many plants)

**Design Applications:**
- Orient patios and gathering spaces for morning sun and afternoon shade
- Place heat-loving plants on south/west exposures
- Use buildings, walls, and evergreen screens to create **warm microclimates** in cold regions
- Use shade trees on west sides to reduce cooling loads

**Microclimate Factors:**

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Topography (hills, valleys) | Cold air pools in low spots (frost pockets); hilltops are windier |
| Water bodies | Moderate temperature extremes; increase local humidity |
| Paving & masonry | Absorb and re-radiate heat (heat-island effect) |
| Vegetation | Transpiration cools air; windbreaks reduce wind chill; canopy shades ground |
| Walls & fences | Create sheltered pockets; reflect heat or block wind |

### 4.2 Topography & Grading

**Slope Analysis:**

| Slope % | Character | Suitable Use |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2% | Flat to nearly flat | Lawns, sports fields, plazas (needs positive drainage) |
| 2–5% | Gentle | Comfortable walking; ideal for most landscape uses |
| 5–10% | Moderate | Walkable with paths; mowing becomes difficult above 8% |
| 10–25% | Steep | Steps or switchback paths required; erosion risk |
| 25%+ | Very steep | Preserve natural vegetation; structural retaining may be needed |

**Grading Principles:**
- **Minimum 1–2% slope** away from buildings for positive drainage
- **Cut and fill** should balance on-site to avoid importing/exporting soil
- Preserve existing **mature trees** — avoid grade changes within the drip line
- Grading shapes the **spatial experience** — berms create enclosure, swales channel water and the eye

### 4.3 Drainage & Hydrology

- **Surface drainage** — Sheet flow, swales, channels, and catch basins
- **Subsurface drainage** — French drains, perforated pipes, drain tiles
- **Stormwater management** — Rain gardens, bioswales, permeable paving, green roofs, detention/retention basins
- **The 100-year floodplain** — Never build in it; use it for open space, trails, and habitat
- **Soil permeability** — Sandy soils drain fast; clay soils drain slow — design accordingly

### 4.4 Wind Patterns & Shelter

- Identify **prevailing wind directions** by season
- Use **windbreaks** (tree belts, walls, berms) to shelter outdoor living areas
- A windbreak protects a zone **10–15× its height** downwind
- **Channeling effects** — Buildings and landforms can accelerate wind in gaps (Venturi effect)
- In hot climates, orient openings to catch **cooling breezes**

### 4.5 Views & Visual Analysis

- **Preserve and frame** significant views (distant mountains, water, landmarks)
- **Screen** undesirable views (parking lots, utilities, neighboring structures)
- **Borrowed landscape** — Incorporate views of surrounding scenery into the site's design (Japanese design concept: *shakkei*)
- **Viewshed analysis** — Map what is visible from key vantage points; used in scenic protection and siting

### 4.6 Soil Analysis for Planting

Key soil properties to evaluate:

| Property | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| **Texture** (sand/silt/clay ratio) | Affects drainage, nutrient-holding capacity, workability |
| **Structure** | Determines root penetration and water movement |
| **pH** | Affects nutrient availability; most plants prefer 6.0–7.0 |
| **Organic matter** | Improves structure, water-holding, and fertility |
| **Depth to bedrock / water table** | Limits root depth and tree size |
| **Compaction** | Common on construction sites; kills root growth; must be remediated |
| **Contamination** | Urban/industrial sites may need soil testing for heavy metals |

## 5. Planning Frameworks

### 5.1 The Site Planning Process

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  1. PROJECT INITIATION                          │
│     • Define goals, scope, budget, schedule     │
│     • Identify stakeholders                     │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  2. SITE INVENTORY                              │
│     • Collect data: survey, soils, ecology,     │
│       utilities, zoning, history                │
│     • Base map preparation                      │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  3. SITE ANALYSIS                               │
│     • SWOT analysis (Strengths, Weaknesses,     │
│       Opportunities, Threats)                   │
│     • Constraint & opportunity mapping          │
│     • Composite suitability analysis            │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  4. PROGRAM DEVELOPMENT                         │
│     • Define uses, spaces, and relationships    │
│     • Quantify areas, capacities, and budgets   │
│     • User needs assessment                     │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  5. CONCEPT / SCHEMATIC DESIGN                  │
│     • Bubble diagrams → concept plans           │
│     • Explore alternative layouts               │
│     • Client/stakeholder review                 │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  6. DESIGN DEVELOPMENT                          │
│     • Refine layout, materials, planting        │
│     • Preliminary grading & drainage plans      │
│     • Cost estimates                            │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  7. CONSTRUCTION DOCUMENTS                      │
│     • Detailed plans, sections, details, specs  │
│     • Permits and approvals                     │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  8. CONSTRUCTION ADMINISTRATION                 │
│     • Bidding, contractor selection             │
│     • Site observation & quality control        │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  9. POST-CONSTRUCTION                           │
│     • Establishment period maintenance          │
│     • Performance monitoring & adaptive mgmt    │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### 5.2 Site Inventory Checklist

**Natural Systems:**
- [ ] Topography / contour survey (1' or 2' intervals)
- [ ] Soils map and on-site soil tests
- [ ] Hydrology: streams, wetlands, floodplains, drainage patterns
- [ ] Vegetation: tree survey (species, size, condition), plant communities
- [ ] Wildlife habitat assessment
- [ ] Climate data: temperature, precipitation, wind rose, sun angles
- [ ] Geology and bedrock

**Built Environment:**
- [ ] Existing structures, paving, walls, fences
- [ ] Utilities: water, sewer, gas, electric, telecom (above and below grade)
- [ ] Circulation: roads, paths, parking, access points
- [ ] Adjacent land uses and context

**Regulatory & Cultural:**
- [ ] Zoning, setbacks, easements, covenants
- [ ] Historic and cultural resources
- [ ] Environmental regulations (wetland buffers, endangered species)
- [ ] Views and visual character

### 5.3 Program Development

The **program** defines what the design must accomplish:

- **Use Zones** — What activities will occur? (passive recreation, active recreation, circulation, parking, service, habitat preservation)
- **Space Requirements** — How much area does each use need?
- **Adjacency & Separation** — Which uses should be near each other? Which must be separated?
- **User Profiles** — Who will use the site? (ages, abilities, peak times)
- **Performance Criteria** — Measurable goals (e.g., manage 100-year storm on-site, provide 50% tree canopy)

### 5.4 Master Planning

A **master plan** provides a long-term vision for a site or campus, typically implemented in phases:

- **Phasing** — Prioritize by need, budget, and logical construction sequence
- **Infrastructure first** — Roads, utilities, and grading set the framework for everything else
- **Quick wins** — Early phases should include visible, high-impact improvements
- **Flexibility** — Plans must accommodate future changes in use, climate, and community needs
- **Living document** — Master plans should be revisited and updated every 5–10 years

## 6. Landscape Ecology

### 6.1 Forman's Patch-Corridor-Matrix Model

Richard Forman's model describes the three fundamental elements of every landscape:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                   M A T R I X                   │
│           (dominant land cover type)            │
│                                                 │
│     ┌──────────┐         ┌──────────┐          │
│     │  PATCH   │─────────│  PATCH   │          │
│     │ (habitat │ CORRIDOR│ (habitat │          │
│     │  island) │ (linear │  island) │          │
│     └──────────┘ connect)└──────────┘          │
│                                                 │
│         ┌──────────┐                            │
│         │  PATCH   │                            │
│         │ (habitat │                            │
│         │  island) │                            │
│         └──────────┘                            │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

| Element | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Patch** | A relatively homogeneous area differing from its surroundings | Forest fragment, wetland, meadow, park |
| **Corridor** | A narrow strip connecting patches | Stream buffer, hedgerow, greenway, road verge |
| **Matrix** | The dominant, most connected landscape element | Agricultural land, suburban development, forest |

### 6.2 Planning Principles from Landscape Ecology

1. **Preserve large patches** — Large, contiguous natural areas support more species and ecological processes than the same area fragmented into small patches
2. **Maintain corridors** — Connect patches to allow species movement and genetic exchange
3. **Minimize road impacts** — Roads fragment habitat and cause wildlife mortality; minimize roads in and near large natural areas
4. **Buffer sensitive areas** — Provide transition zones between development and natural areas
5. **Work with the matrix** — Even the dominant developed landscape can be made more permeable to wildlife through native plantings, green corridors, and reduced barriers

## 7. Sustainability & Modern Standards

### 7.1 The SITES Rating System (Sustainable SITES Initiative)

Administered by the Green Business Certification Inc. (GBCI) — the landscape equivalent of LEED for buildings.

**Rating Categories:**

| Category | Focus |
|---|---|
| **Site Context** | Understanding surrounding context, ecosystem services |
| **Pre-Design Assessment & Planning** | Site inventory, stakeholder engagement, performance goals |
| **Water** | Stormwater management, water conservation, water quality |
| **Soil & Vegetation** | Soil health, native plants, invasive species management, urban heat reduction |
| **Materials** | Sustainable sourcing, recycled content, regional materials, reducing waste |
| **Human Health & Well-Being** | Outdoor activity, mental restoration, accessibility, safety |
| **Construction** | Minimizing disturbance, controlling erosion, protecting existing features |
| **Operations & Maintenance** | Adaptive management, reduced inputs, organic practices |
| **Education & Performance Monitoring** | Public engagement, long-term performance tracking |
| **Innovation** | Exemplary performance beyond standard credits |

**Certification Levels (out of 200 points):**
- **Certified** — 70 points
- **Silver** — 85 points
- **Gold** — 100 points
- **Platinum** — 135 points

### 7.2 Sustainable Design Strategies

- **Green Infrastructure** — Rain gardens, bioswales, green roofs, permeable paving, constructed wetlands
- **Low-Impact Development (LID)** — Manage stormwater at the source; mimic predevelopment hydrology
- **Xeriscaping** — Water-efficient landscaping using drought-tolerant and native plants
- **Urban Forestry** — Strategic tree planting for shade, air quality, carbon sequestration, and stormwater interception
- **Soil Restoration** — De-compact, amend, and protect soils as living systems
- **Adaptive Reuse** — Repurpose existing materials, structures, and infrastructure
- **Dark Sky Design** — Minimize light pollution through shielded, low-intensity, warm-toned lighting

## 8. Glossary of Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Biome** | A large-scale ecological community defined by climate and dominant vegetation (e.g., temperate deciduous forest) |
| **Bioswale** | A vegetated channel designed to filter and convey stormwater runoff |
| **Borrowed Landscape (Shakkei)** | Incorporating distant scenery into a site's visual composition |
| **Charrette** | An intensive, collaborative design workshop with stakeholders |
| **Climax Community** | The stable, mature ecological community that develops through succession |
| **Cut and Fill** | Earthwork that removes soil (cut) from high areas and places it (fill) in low areas to achieve desired grades |
| **DBH** | Diameter at Breast Height — standard tree measurement taken at 4.5 feet above ground |
| **Drip Line** | The outermost extent of a tree's canopy; the critical root protection zone |
| **Easement** | A legal right to use another's land for a specific purpose (utility, access, conservation) |
| **Ecotone** | A transitional zone between two ecological communities |
| **Genius Loci** | The spirit or character of a place |
| **Grading** | The shaping of land surface to achieve desired slopes and drainage |
| **Green Infrastructure** | A network of natural and designed landscape features that provide ecosystem services |
| **Hardscape** | Non-living landscape elements: paving, walls, structures |
| **Microclimate** | The climate of a small, specific area as modified by local conditions |
| **Overlay Analysis** | McHarg's method of layering mapped data to determine land-use suitability |
| **Rain Garden** | A shallow planted depression that captures and infiltrates stormwater |
| **Riparian** | Relating to the banks of a stream, river, or other water body |
| **Setback** | The minimum distance a structure must be from a property line, road, or other feature |
| **Softscape** | Living landscape elements: plants, turf, soil |
| **Stormwater BMP** | Best Management Practice for controlling runoff quantity and quality |
| **Succession** | The natural process of ecological community change over time |
| **Swale** | A shallow, vegetated depression that channels surface water |
| **Viewshed** | The area visible from a specific vantage point |
| **Watershed** | The total land area that drains to a given point on a waterway |
| **Xeriscape** | A landscape designed to minimize water use through appropriate plant selection and design |
