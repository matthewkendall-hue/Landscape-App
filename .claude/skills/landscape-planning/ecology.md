# Ecological Planning

## McHarg's Ecological Method

The ecological planning process:
1. INVENTORY — Collect data on all natural systems
2. OVERLAY — Layer maps of each system transparently
3. ANALYSIS — Identify constraints, opportunities, and conflicts
4. SUITABILITY — Determine which uses are appropriate where
5. DESIGN — Shape development to fit ecological capacity

Data Layers for Overlay Analysis:
- Bedrock geology and surficial geology
- Physiography and slope analysis
- Hydrology (surface water, groundwater, floodplains, wetlands)
- Soils (type, depth, drainage, bearing capacity, agricultural value)
- Vegetation (forest types, successional stages, rare communities)
- Wildlife habitats and movement corridors
- Climate and microclimate (sun, wind, precipitation patterns)
- Land use history and cultural resources
- Visual / scenic quality

## Ecological Succession and Disturbance

- Primary succession — Colonization of bare substrate (rock, sand)
- Secondary succession — Regrowth after disturbance (fire, clearing)
- Climax community — Stable endpoint community for a given climate and soil
- Disturbance regimes — Fire, flooding, wind, grazing are natural processes that maintain diversity; good design accommodates rather than suppresses them

## Watershed-Based Planning

Water is the organizing principle of landscape:
- Every site sits within a watershed (drainage basin)
- Development upstream affects flooding, water quality, and habitat downstream
- Best practice: plan at the watershed scale, not the property-line scale
- Key strategies: preserve riparian buffers, manage stormwater at source, protect recharge areas, daylight buried streams

## Habitat Connectivity

- Wildlife corridors connect habitat patches; allow species movement, genetic exchange, migration
- Fragmentation (roads, development) is one of greatest threats to biodiversity
- Riparian corridors (streamside zones) serve as natural connectors
- Design principle: maintain and restore connections between large habitat areas

## Native Plant Communities and Biomes

- Use regionally native plants adapted to local soils, climate, and wildlife
- Understand site biome (temperate forest, grassland, desert, etc.) and plant hardiness zone
- Design plant communities, not individual plants — mimic structure (canopy, understory, shrub layer, ground layer)
- Benefits: lower maintenance, better wildlife habitat, resilience to climate stress
