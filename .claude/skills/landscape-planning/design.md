# Design Principles

## Core Visual and Spatial Principles

- Unity — All elements feel as one coherent composition. Use repetition of materials, colors, forms; consistent design vocabulary.
- Balance — Visual weight evenly distributed. Symmetrical (formal, mirror-image) or Asymmetrical (informal, achieved through contrast).
- Proportion — Size relationships between all features. Relate plant masses, paving areas, structures to human scale and each other.
- Rhythm — Repetition creating visual movement. Repeated plantings, paving patterns, structural elements draw the eye forward.
- Emphasis / Focal Point — Visual hierarchy identifying what matters most. Specimen trees, water features, sculpture, framed views.
- Contrast — Juxtaposition of opposites. Texture (fine vs. coarse), color (warm vs. cool), form (upright vs. spreading).
- Simplicity — Restraint in elements and materials. Few well-chosen materials create stronger designs.

## Spatial Enclosure and Outdoor Rooms

Creating defined outdoor rooms:
- Floor — Ground plane (lawn, paving, groundcover, water)
- Walls — Vertical elements (hedges, walls, fences, shrub masses, berms)
- Ceiling — Overhead plane (tree canopy, pergola, arbor, sky)

Degrees of Enclosure:
- Fully Enclosed (courtyard) — intimate, sheltered
- Partially Enclosed (woodland glade) — comfortable, defined
- Semi-Open (meadow) — spacious
- Fully Open (open field) — expansive, exposed

People feel most comfortable in spaces where enclosure is proportionate to purpose — intimate for seating, more open for active recreation.

## Sequence and Movement

Designing movement through landscape over time:
- Approach — How one arrives; transition from public to private
- Threshold — Moment of entry; gateway, change in material, spatial compression
- Procession — Journey through series of spaces with varying character
- Arrival / Climax — Destination; often the most dramatic space or view
- Conclusion — Exit; resolution or return

Techniques:
- Compression and release (narrow path opening to broad vista)
- Concealment and revelation (hiding view until specific vantage point)
- Change of level (steps, ramps, bridges alter perception)
- Change of material (gravel to stone to lawn signals different zones)

## Color Theory in Planting Design

- Warm colors (red, orange, yellow) — advance toward viewer; energizing; best for focal points
- Cool colors (blue, violet, green) — recede; calming; create depth
- Monochromatic schemes — Variations of one hue; elegant and restful
- Complementary schemes — Opposite hues (purple + gold); vibrant, high-contrast
- Analogous schemes — Adjacent hues (blue + violet + magenta); harmonious
- Foliage color is more permanent than flower color and should form the design backbone
- Seasonal shifts — Plan for spring bloom, summer fullness, autumn color, winter structure (bark, evergreens, seed heads)

## Temporal Design — The Fourth Dimension

Landscapes change through:
- Hours — Sun and shadow patterns, lighting, scent (night-blooming flowers)
- Seasons — Bloom succession, foliage color, deciduous vs. evergreen, snow loading
- Years — Plant growth and maturation; 5-year landscape differs vastly from 20-year
- Decades — Ecological succession; infrastructure aging; cultural shifts in use

Design for the long game — select plants and materials that improve with age.
